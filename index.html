Sidharth Rony

Researcher, UNU-MERIT, Maastricht University
Ph.D. in Economics, Royal Holloway, University of London

I am a labour economist and data scientist. My research measures technological change, automation, artificial intelligence, and the green and digital transition, and studies how it reshapes skills, tasks, wages, and the structure of work. I build these measures from large-scale text using natural language processing and large language models, and I pair them with quasi-experimental designs.

Fields: labour economics, economics of innovation, text as data.

Research

Job market paper

LLM Meets Job Advertisements: Unmasking Skill Premia in the UKJMP
Forthcoming, Research in Labor Economics, Vol. 54. UNU-MERIT Working Paper 2026-010.
PDFWorking paper

Rapid advances in technology and events such as COVID-19 have significantly transformed the modern workplace, potentially altering the skills demanded in jobs. This study examines the evolving demand and posted-wage premia for Information and Communication Technology (ICT), interpersonal, and Artificial Intelligence (AI) skills in the UK labour market. Using a comprehensive dataset of online job advertisements (2016 to 2022), skills are extracted and categorised via GPT-4 zero-shot learning. Cross-sectional log-wage regressions, incorporating occupation and regional fixed effects with three-way clustered standard errors, reveal divergent trends in skill compensation. While interpersonal skills are ubiquitously demanded (approximately 90% of listings), they yield no significant posted-wage premium. In contrast, ICT skills, demanded in approximately 55% of postings, carry a posted-wage premium of approximately 7%. AI skills, mentioned in approximately 3% of postings, carry a posted-wage premium of approximately 9% within the ICT-mentioning subsample.

Working papers

How Regions Use Large Language Models
with Alexandra Badort (TU Munich), 2026. Draft available upon request.

A widespread view holds that the adoption of large language models (LLMs) follows capability at two levels: models are used for the tasks they perform well (machine capability), and the regions whose own capabilities match those tasks (regional capabilities) use them most and take up each improvement first. We test this view on four waves of US state-level data on Anthropic’s Claude, sampled between August 2025 and May 2026, spanning three releases that improved coding. Skill-based measures of regional capabilities built from job postings are the main regressors, alongside the occupational composition of state employment. Regional capabilities are strongly associated with how intensively a state uses the model. Much of this is shared with income and GDP, but skill relatedness survives every control, and in the 2026 data it also appears in Microsoft’s estimates of AI users across assistants. Regional capabilities are not associated with what the model is used for: use follows local occupational composition only weakly, and no more strongly where regional capabilities are deeper. Nor are they associated with how augmentatively states use the model, or with which states gained share of use after the releases; nationally, coding lost more than a third of its share while education and business tasks gained. Because more than nine in ten state–task counts are unpublished, the extensive margin is not identified, and an imputation that fits the published counts overstates the unpublished ones.
